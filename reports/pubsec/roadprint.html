How to read this road

Each position is read on a five-point scale — Reactive: Work happens only as reaction, if at all — no reliable artifact, owner, practice, or implementation evidence. Inconsistent: Recorded intent, a draft artifact, or occasional execution exists, but the practice does not yet operate reliably. Defined: The practice is defined and owned — a named owner or operator attests it operates — with limited or indirect evidence. Consistent: The practice runs consistently, supported by telemetry, tooling, or operational evidence. Automated: Reusable / automated evidence that supports cATO evidence expectations without asserting authorization. A position below the top is a step; a step at Reactive moves first to Defined, every other step moves to Automated.
Guardrails are cited in the authority order: floor and peer evidence before patterns. A provider's own account of its architecture never outranks the floor.
Conclusions rest on a deliberate hierarchy, and the order is what makes a finding survive a standards-literate reader.

Tier 1 — the floor, required authority: RMF (NIST SP 800-37 / DoDI 8510.01), the DoD Cloud SRG and its impact-level boundary requirements, SCCA shared components, DoD Zero Trust direction, and continuous-authorization pillar doctrine. What must be true. In the reference corpus, F-codes.

Tier 2 — the known road, peer-program evidence: Army ECMA / cARMY shared services; USAF Cloud One's program-scoped inheritance; USAF Platform One's platform authorization; Navy Black Pearl's inherited authorization; Navy Flank Speed Zero Trust operations; Army CAMO / CloudTracker FinOps results. G-codes.

Tier 3 — target-state patterns: well-architected and landing-zone guidance, SRE practice, platform-delivered control inheritance. N-codes. A provider's own account of its architecture never outranks Tier 1 in a written finding.

Every claim is cited to a stated, public source; treat a cited code as a pointer, not a proof, and for contractual or authorization-relevant claims cite the primary source, never the summary.

Start well

A mission organization moving to cloud at scale is not short of information; it is short of focus. Four enterprise programs have already walked the road — Army ECMA / cARMY, USAF Cloud One, USAF Platform One, Navy Black Pearl — so the swim lanes, gates, interdependencies, skills and operating model are known and sourced ground. What a client needs is not discovery. What is not portable is fit: the knowledge travels, the sequence does not. Foundations turns known ground into a sequence this client can actually consume.

Its base product is the transformation storyboard: the client's future work organized against one researched map of ten readiness domains unpacking into fifty-three workstreams — twenty-three launching immediately, twenty-nine production gates that other work cannot correctly precede. The map is invariant; that is the trust argument. What changes are three overlays: where they are today (from their own evidence), the workstreams that move them, and day-two operations — the shared and mission services that keep the result running, and whose they are.

Every workstream is classified by the kind of work it actually is — process and documentation, technology acquisition, sourcing, or engineering build — because those four answers do not share an owner or a calendar, and a storyboard that blurs them produces a plan nobody can fund.

Migration is an event. Operating is a posture. The piece most plans skip is the day-two operating concept: who runs what, once it is running. Shared services can be the client's own, acquired, or inherited from a larger program they join — each putting accountability in a different place. Mission services sit on top, inside a boundary somebody has to own.

Before any workload moves, one question needs a name attached to it: who owns the incident at zero three hundred? Naming that early is what keeps year two from surprising the sponsor. The day-two operating model is a first-class input to sequencing, not a downstream consequence.
