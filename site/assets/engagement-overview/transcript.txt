Cloud Waypoint — engagement overview
AI-generated narration. Studio and laboratory examples are fictional.

Arrive ready.
A landing zone can take months to authorize. Your applications don't have to wait for it. Cloud Waypoint is a readiness program for missions adopting cloud through JWCC, so the mission arrives ready when the environment does.

Use the runway.
The readiness work runs alongside landing-zone delivery, not after it. Foundations takes about eight weeks, the two advance weeks of the data call included. Dependency mapping runs about twelve, and launch preparation overlaps both. The whole shape is about twenty weeks. That timing is illustrative, not a promise.

Find the real starting point.
It starts with a three-hour working session on the mission, the estate, the boundary, and current readiness. The workshop finds the right starting path. It does not choose your operating model. That decision stays with the mission.

Design the environment applications will enter.
Foundations turns mission knowledge and existing artifacts into a proposed general support system: its boundary, its shared services, its security and identity layers, and an owner for each.

Observed does not mean approved.
Dependency mapping shows how workloads actually communicate. Owners review what was seen and confirm what is needed. The result becomes migration waves and authorization inputs, built around application communities rather than isolated servers.

Design day two before day one.
A sound environment still fails without clear ownership. So day two is designed first: who monitors, who approves change, who responds to incidents, and who keeps the evidence current.

Standards guide the questions.
The standards behind the method are public. The Reference Studio indexes the governing sources, from the DoD Cloud Computing Security Requirements Guide to the Risk Management Framework, each with its publisher, its edition, and when it was last verified.

And what must not be inferred.
Search an impact level and every result says what its source supports, and what it doesn't. A listed service still needs its authorization-scope checks, and an impact-level label alone doesn't choose an environment.

Where the model stops, and a person decides.
The GULF laboratory uses a fictional specimen to show how an authorization package comes together, and exactly where the model stops so a named official can decide.

A real process, not a web page.
The method runs in a real application. This is the NatSec studio working a fictional engagement: one record, seven stages, and every fact traced back to its source.

Nine decisions, each with an owner.
Discovery feeds the applications register and the dependency map. The general support system is settled through nine decisions, each checked against its evidence and owned by a named person.

Every package. One record.
Every deliverable prints from that same record: the executive brief, the transformation plan, the draft security plan. They arrive as packages, the midpoint snapshot at week four, then the readiness, transformation and final packages at week eight, each sized for its reader, each tracing back to the same evidence.

Cloud Waypoint does not compete with the landing zone.
Cloud Waypoint doesn't sell the cloud, replace your provider, or grant authorization. It builds the readiness between cloud access and mission adoption, alongside whoever delivers the environment.

Know what you are moving. Design where it is going. Build security into the plan.
Evidence informs. Named individuals with authority decide, and the authorizing official keeps every authorization decision. To talk about your mission, visit cloud-waypoint dot com.
